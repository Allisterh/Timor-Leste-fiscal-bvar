\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Data-limited economies often combine annual national accounts with quarterly indicators or temporal disaggregation before estimating fiscal models. Using the quarterly indicator for Timor-Leste published by INETL from 2014Q1 to 2023Q4, we estimate local projections as our preferred baseline and a Bayesian VAR as a robustness check. Local projections estimate import responses between $-0.28\%$ and $0.24\%$ across horizons; most intervals include zero, and the few precisely estimated values are small and alternate in sign. The BVAR estimates $-0.07\%$ at impact and essentially zero thereafter. Both are an order of magnitude smaller than the long-sample benchmark. The INETL indicator is itself benchmarked to annual GDP with quarterly indicators, so the relevant comparison is with a long 2002--2023 sample built by univariate Denton--Cholette disaggregation, not with raw quarterly data. In that long sample, the LP response peaks at 5.5\% after three quarters and the BVAR response is 0.91\% at impact. A 2x2 hybrid design on the common 2014--2023 window shows that neither replacing the fiscal series nor replacing the outcome series with its interpolated counterpart reproduces the long-sample result. The long-sample result is therefore not a mechanical consequence of interpolation alone: it is associated with earlier observations, is sensitive to the event-dummy specification, and is not robust in the common window. We recommend that fiscal-leakage estimates in data-limited economies use indicator-based quarterly data for inference, treat long interpolated samples as historical benchmarks, and report common-window tests alongside the main estimates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Timor-Leste is a small, open, petroleum-rich economy with a short macroeconomic history. Government expenditure finances state-building, infrastructure, and a large share of domestic absorption, while the productive base remains narrow. A central policy question is therefore whether fiscal expansion supports domestic non-oil activity or leaks abroad through imports. Because the country's national accounts are available at annual frequency for most of the sample, quarterly estimates necessarily depend on some form of temporal disaggregation. The question is which construction, and which sample window, is informative about fiscal transmission.

This paper studies that question with the quarterly indicator published by the Instituto Nacional de Estatística (INETL) from 2014 onwards. The indicator is not a raw quarterly survey: INETL replicates annual GDP and then disaggregates it using quarterly indicators \parencite{inetl2023}. We use it to estimate quarterly local projections (LP) for 2014Q1--2023Q4, with a Bayesian VAR (BVAR) as a robustness check, and we compare the results with those obtained from a long 2002--2023 sample constructed by univariate Denton--Cholette disaggregation. The comparison therefore isolates the incremental effect of univariate interpolation and the sample window; it is not a comparison between raw and constructed data.

The main finding is that the strong leakage result is fragile. In the INETL sample, the baseline local-projection estimates imply import responses between $-0.28\%$ and $0.24\%$ across horizons. Most intervals include zero, and the few precisely estimated values are small and alternate in sign. A three-variable BVAR, reported as a robustness check, is stable and estimates an import response of $-0.07\%$ at impact, $0.04\%$ after three quarters, and essentially zero thereafter; its 68\% interval includes zero at all horizons. Both estimates are economically negligible relative to the long-sample benchmark. Non-oil GDP shows a positive impact response in both approaches, but no persistent positive response. The five-variable INETL BVAR, which adds the CPI and private credit, is unstable because the short sample cannot support the additional dynamics; this instability is itself informative about the limits of the quarterly indicator.

In the long interpolated sample, the LP estimates are larger, peaking near 5.5\% after three quarters, and the BVAR estimates a significant import response of 0.91\% at impact, declining to 0.59\% after six quarters and 0.25\% after twelve. The long-sample results reproduce the earlier finding that imports respond more strongly than non-oil GDP, although the BVAR response is front-loaded rather than hump-shaped. The contrast between the two samples is stark, and it survives alternative orderings and seasonal adjustment choices. It does not, however, survive a common-window test: in a 2x2 design that varies the fiscal series and the outcome series separately over 2014--2023, no cell reproduces the leakage. Interpolation alone is therefore not sufficient to generate the long-sample result.

The contribution is threefold. First, the paper provides LP and BVAR estimates for Timor-Leste based on the INETL quarterly indicator, while making explicit that the indicator is itself benchmarked and disaggregated. We use local projections as the preferred baseline because they estimate each horizon directly, handle the event dummies without relying on a full-system prior, and make the horizon-specific controls transparent; the BVAR is a robustness check on the full dynamics. Second, the paper documents the fragility of the long-sample leakage result and develops a simple 2x2 decomposition that separates fiscal interpolation, outcome interpolation, and the sample window. This decomposition shows that the earlier mechanism was too broad: long interpolated samples can produce estimates that are not reproduced in a common quarterly window, but interpolation by itself does not mechanically generate them. Third, it offers a practical recommendation for policy analysis in data-limited economies: use indicator-based quarterly data for inference, treat long interpolated samples as historical benchmarks rather than policy counterfactuals, and report common-window tests whenever the data permit.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the literature. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and the construction of the long sample. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the empirical strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results and robustness checks. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The macroeconomic effects of fiscal policy have been extensively studied, but consensus on the size of fiscal multipliers remains elusive, especially in developing and resource-rich economies. \textcite{blanchard2002} introduced the structural VAR approach to identify government spending shocks and showed that spending increases typically raise output. \textcite{mountford2009} used sign restrictions to show that fiscal effects depend on the type of shock and the state of the economy. For small open economies, the literature emphasizes that multipliers tend to be smaller because part of the stimulus is absorbed by imports. \textcite{ilzetzki2013} find lower multipliers in economies with more open trade regimes and more flexible exchange rates. Evidence for Southeast Asia, including ASEAN member states, points in the same direction: fiscal multipliers are typically modest and sensitive to the import content of spending \parencite{tang2013,dime2021}; a calibration for Cambodia also implies a low first-year multiplier \parencite{baksa2020}. Resource-rich economies face additional challenges: commodity-price volatility and Dutch-disease pressures complicate the conduct of fiscal policy \parencite{frankel2012}.

Vector autoregressions are the standard tool for dynamic multivariate analysis, but short time series make unrestricted OLS estimates unreliable. Bayesian VARs, introduced to macroeconomics by \textcite{litterman1986} and refined by \textcite{giannone2015}, impose prior beliefs that shrink the parameter space and can produce stable responses even with limited observations. The \texttt{BVAR} package of \textcite{bvar2021} implements hierarchical prior selection for these models. Local projections estimate horizon-specific responses without imposing the full VAR dynamics \parencite{jorda2005}, but they are not immune to small-sample imprecision: with short samples they can be sensitive to lag length and controls. We therefore use local projections as a transparent baseline and report the BVAR as a robustness check rather than claiming that either estimator dominates in small samples.

A separate literature addresses the construction of high-frequency data from annual benchmarks. \textcite{denton1971} proposed a quadratic minimization method, extended by \textcite{cholette1984}; \textcite{saxsteiner2013} and the \texttt{tempdisagg} package implement these methods in R. Temporal disaggregation is widely used in data-poor settings, but it is not a source of new information: it distributes an annual total using a smoothness criterion or a related indicator. The resulting quarterly series are mechanically smooth and share the annual benchmark with the other disaggregated variables. This paper asks whether such shared smoothness is sufficient to generate spurious fiscal transmission. The answer is negative: shared smoothness can distort estimates, but its effect depends on the sample window, so it cannot be inferred from a long interpolated sample alone.

Studies of fiscal policy in Timor-Leste are scarce, but the available estimates point to small multipliers. \textcite{worldbank2021} estimates an impact multiplier of 0.17 and a cumulative multiplier of 0.08 using a quarterly SVAR for 2010--2019 with real quarterly data. \textcite{imf2024} reports multipliers of 0.1--0.2, attributing the small size to the low quality and high import content of public spending. These estimates are consistent with the small responses obtained from the INETL indicator below, and they are difficult to reconcile with the large leakage implied by the long interpolated sample. This paper contributes by combining the INETL quarterly indicator with a transparent comparison to the interpolated sample, and by showing that the long-sample result is fragile in a common-window design.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The INETL quarterly indicator}
\label{sec:official}

The baseline data are the quarterly indicator published by INETL for 2004--2023 \parencite{inetl2023}. We use the expenditure table (Table~7.1) at constant 2015 prices for 2014Q1--2023Q4, the period for which quarterly observations are available. The sample contains 40 quarters. The variables are general government final consumption expenditure, non-oil GDP by expenditure, and imports of goods and services. The consumer price index is obtained from the monthly DNE series and averaged to quarterly frequency \parencite{dne_cpi}. Credit to the private sector is the BCTL monetary survey series, measured at the end of each quarter \parencite{bctl2024}.

A crucial qualification is that the INETL series are not raw quarterly observations. INETL estimates quarterly GDP by replicating the annual expenditure accounts and then temporally disaggregating them using quarterly indicators \parencite{inetl2023}. The indicator therefore contains more high-frequency information than a univariate Denton--Cholette allocation, but it is also benchmarked, model-based, and potentially measured with error. We treat it as the best available quarterly indicator, not as error-free truth. This distinction matters for the interpretation of the comparison: the long sample differs from the baseline in both its sample window and the amount of quarterly indicator information used in the disaggregation.

The indicator series are seasonally adjusted with X-13ARIMA-SEATS before estimation. The baseline uses the seasonally adjusted data; an unadjusted specification with quarterly dummies is reported as a robustness check. Table~\ref{tab:variables} summarizes the variables and sources.

\subsection{Data validation and source differences}
\label{sec:validation}

The INETL quarterly GDP and import series aggregate almost exactly to the annual ADB series \parencite{adb2025}. Over 2014--2023, the largest absolute difference between the sum of the four quarterly observations and the annual figure is 0.85 million USD for GDP (2016) and 1.10 million USD for imports (2021). These differences are negligible relative to annual levels of about 1,500--1,650 million USD for GDP and 730--980 million USD for imports.

Two source differences require attention. First, INETL General Government FCE is systematically lower than the ADB government expenditure series. The gap ranges from 180.5 million USD in 2014 to 123.9 million USD in 2023 and matches the on-budget Development and Technical Assistance component that the ADB reports separately. The ADB broad aggregate is therefore the sum of the narrow national-accounts measure and this component; Table~\ref{tab:gov_decomposition} reports the reconciliation. This matters for the interpretation of the hybrid design: the broader fiscal measure is not an arbitrary alternative, but the ADB aggregate used in the long-sample benchmark. Second, the BCTL domestic credit series matches the ADB private credit series until 2017, but is about 50 million USD lower from 2018 to 2022 and 6 million USD lower in 2023. The break is consistent with a definitional change in the monetary survey. We use the BCTL series for the INETL baseline and report the discrepancy rather than forcing the two sources to match. \subsection{The long interpolated sample}
\label{sec:long}

The long sample covers 2002Q1--2023Q4 (88 quarters) and serves as the comparison benchmark. Annual series from the ADB Key Indicators \parencite{adb2025} are disaggregated with the univariate Denton--Cholette proportional method \parencite{denton1971,cholette1984,saxsteiner2013,tempdisagg}. For flows, the annual sum constraint is imposed; for the CPI and credit, the quarterly average constraint is imposed. The quarterly import indicator from Trade Map \parencite{trademap2025} was tested in a Chow--Lin specification but was not statistically significant (coefficient 0.171, standard error 0.296, $p=0.571$), so the univariate method was retained. The long sample includes five event dummies: the 2006 political crisis, the 2009 global financial crisis, the 2012--2014 transition, the 2016--2018 political crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

The key feature of the long sample is that all real series are smooth by construction. Table~\ref{tab:volatility} compares the standard deviation of quarterly log changes over the common 2014--2023 period. For government expenditure, the INETL indicator is about 15 times more volatile than the interpolated series; for GDP, about 14 times; for imports, about 4 times. The indicator contains high-frequency variation that univariate interpolation removes, although part of that variation is itself model-based. This difference is central to the interpretation of the results, but it is not sufficient to identify the source of the long-sample leakage.

\section{Empirical strategy}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Local projections: preferred baseline}
\label{sec:lp}

Local projections are our preferred baseline because they estimate each horizon directly, handle the event dummies without relying on a full-system prior, and make the horizon-specific controls transparent \parencite{jorda2005}. We estimate a three-variable VAR with the same variables, lags, and dummies, extract the recursively identified government expenditure shock, and project each outcome on the shock and its pre-determined controls:
\[
y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h \varepsilon_t + \Gamma_h X_{t-1} + u_{t+h},
\]
where $X_{t-1}$ contains a constant, two lags of the three core variables, and the event dummies. The coefficient $\beta_h$ is the response at horizon $h$. For government expenditure, imports, and non-oil GDP we implement the IV local-projection estimator of \textcite{adammer2019} using \texttt{lpirfs}; for the CPI and private credit we use the same estimator with the two core lags as exogenous controls, so that all five responses share the same shock and information set. The HAC bandwidth is fixed at six quarters, and standard errors are finite-sample adjusted. The shock is normalized so that the impact response of government expenditure is 1\%, which makes the LP coefficients directly comparable to the BVAR elasticities. We report horizons 0--12.

Local projections are not immune to small-sample problems. With 40 observations, horizon-specific regressions are imprecise, HAC standard errors can over-reject, and estimates can be sensitive to lag length and to the set of controls. We do not claim that LP dominates the BVAR in small samples. We use LP as the preferred baseline because the shock and controls are held fixed and the horizon-specific estimates are transparent; the BVAR provides a second estimator with different shrinkage. The relevant robustness check is that the two estimators agree on the near-zero official response, not that either estimator is uniformly superior. The Monte Carlo exercise in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} calibrates the size of the LP test, and the confidence intervals make the imprecision explicit.

\subsection{Bayesian VAR: robustness check}
\label{sec:bvar}

As a robustness check, we estimate a three-variable BVAR in logarithms:
\[
y_t = \left(\ln \text{gov}_t,\ \ln \text{imports}_t,\ \ln \text{GDP}_t\right)' ,
\]
with two lags, a constant, and the two event dummies. The \texttt{BVAR} implementation does not accept exogenous regressors; the event dummies are therefore partialled out of the endogenous variables by OLS before estimation, and the BVAR is fitted to the residual series. This is equivalent to conditioning the endogenous dynamics on the event dates and avoids silently omitting the dummies. The variables are ordered with government expenditure first, so the fiscal shock is recursively identified: government expenditure does not respond contemporaneously to imports or GDP, while both can respond to the fiscal shock within the quarter. A second ordering places GDP first and government expenditure second, allowing expenditure to react to activity within the quarter. The comparison between orderings is a sensitivity check, not a test that eliminates all identification assumptions.

The recursive ordering also has an institutional interpretation. Timor-Leste's budget is annual and is executed through cash-based treasury releases; procurement and contracting create lags between appropriation and spending. Government consumption is therefore treated as predetermined within the quarter relative to imports and non-oil GDP: spending decisions are made before contemporaneous import and output outcomes are observed. This is not an assumption that expenditure is exogenous to the economy. It is an assumption about within-quarter timing, and the second ordering relaxes it by placing GDP first. Quarterly budget-execution data are not available for the full sample, so we cannot construct an external fiscal shock; the estimates are conditional on this timing restriction.

The prior is a Minnesota prior with hierarchical selection of the overall tightness $\lambda$. The prior mode of $\lambda$ is 0.2, with support $[0.001,3]$; $\alpha$ has mode 2 and support $[1,3]$; the scale $\psi$ is set to the square root of the variance of the first differences of each series, with the package's default bounds. The sampler uses 100,000 draws, 20,000 burn-in draws, and thinning every 20 draws, leaving 4,000 retained draws. Impulse responses are computed for 12 quarters with 68\% and 90\% credible intervals.

The three-variable specification is chosen because the INETL sample has only 40 observations. A five-variable model that adds the CPI and private credit has a maximum companion modulus above one (Table~\ref{tab:stability}); the additional dynamics are not supported by the data. The five-variable model is reported as a robustness check, not used for inference. The long sample, with 88 observations, supports both the three- and five-variable specifications.

Unit-root tests are reported in Table~\ref{tab:stationarity}. We apply augmented Dickey--Fuller \parencite{dickeyfuller1979}, KPSS \parencite{kpss1992}, and Zivot--Andrews \parencite{zivot1992} tests. The evidence is mixed. ADF tests do not reject a unit root for most series at the 5\% level; KPSS rejects stationarity for the CPI and credit in the official sample and for several series in the long sample; and Zivot--Andrews rejects the unit root for non-oil GDP with a break in 2019Q4 and, in the long sample, for government expenditure and credit. Because the tests disagree, we do not force a binary classification: a series is labelled stationary only if all three tests favour stationarity, a unit root only if none does, and mixed otherwise. Given the short sample and the mixed evidence, we estimate the BVAR in levels with a Minnesota prior, which shrinks toward a random-walk representation and is robust to persistent series. The long-sample tests are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_long}.

The five-variable official BVAR produces qualitatively similar results but is unstable. Its import response is small and the GDP response is short-lived. Because the maximum companion modulus is 1.033, we do not use it for inference. The instability is a symptom of the short sample: with 40 observations and two lags, the five-variable system has more parameters than the data can support. This is not a defect of the estimator; it is a constraint of the available official data.

Taken together, the local projections and the BVAR provide no evidence of a large or persistent import leakage. The LP point estimates, which we treat as the preferred baseline, lie between $-0.28\%$ and $0.24\%$; the few precisely estimated values are small and alternate in sign. The BVAR corroborates this conclusion: it estimates an import response of $-0.07\%$ at impact and below 0.05\% thereafter. Both are an order of magnitude smaller than the long-sample estimates and do not support the view that fiscal stimulus is largely spent abroad. The GDP response is fragile: the BVAR finds a short-lived positive impact effect, and the LP finds a positive impact effect that turns negative at two and three quarters. The robust conclusion is that the INETL data do not identify a large import leakage or a persistent positive output effect.

\subsection{The long-sample benchmark}
\label{sec:long_results}

The long-sample LP estimates are large (Figure~\ref{fig:lp_long} and Table~\ref{tab:lp_elasticities}). The import response rises from 1.1\% at impact to 2.8\% after one quarter, 4.5\% after two quarters, and 5.5\% after three quarters, then declines to 3.2\% after six quarters and 1.4\% after twelve quarters, with the last interval including zero. The GDP response is initially negative and then rises to 0.67\% after six quarters and 0.53\% after twelve. This is the benchmark that the official data fail to reproduce.

\subsection{Why the two samples disagree}
\label{sec:artifact}

The contrast between the INETL and long-sample results is not driven by the estimator, the prior, or the identification. The same three-variable BVAR, with the same lags, prior, and ordering, produces a near-zero import response in the INETL sample and a large, significant response in the long sample. The difference must therefore come from the data: either their construction or the sample period.

A simple 2x2 design separates the two dimensions. Because the earlier leakage estimate came from a BVAR, we implement the decomposition in the BVAR; the LP estimates in Tables~\ref{tab:lp_elasticities} tell the same sample contrast. We estimate four cells over the common 2014--2023 window: the official baseline; Hybrid-F, which replaces official government expenditure with the interpolated ADB broad series while keeping official outcomes; Hybrid-O, which keeps official government expenditure and replaces the outcomes with interpolated series; and Interpolated both, which uses interpolated fiscal and outcome series. All cells use the same three-variable BVAR, two lags, prior, sampler, event dummies, and ordering. Table~\ref{tab:hybrid2x2} reports the import response at horizons 1, 3, 6, and 12. No cell reproduces the large and persistent leakage. Hybrid-F has wide intervals and an import response between $-0.20\%$ and $0.51\%$; its twelve-quarter response is $0.13\%$. Hybrid-O is close to zero at every horizon. Interpolated both is $-0.06\%$ after one quarter and $0.05\%$ after twelve quarters, with intervals that include zero throughout. This is a direct test of the interpolation hypothesis: if joint interpolation were sufficient to manufacture the result, the Interpolated both cell should reproduce it over the common window. It does not.

This reinterpretation qualifies the earlier mechanism. Denton--Cholette does smooth the series and removes high-frequency independent variation (Table~\ref{tab:volatility}), but shared smoothness alone is not enough to produce a large import response in 2014--2023. Conversely, the official data contain independent quarterly variation, yet the absence of leakage is not simply a mechanical consequence of that variation either. The long-sample result is a joint product of the earlier sample and the construction of the data, and the common-window design shows why it should not be generalized.

A final check addresses seasonal adjustment. The unadjusted official model, which uses quarterly dummies instead of X-13 adjustment, produces an import response of $-0.11\%$ at impact and $0.04\%$ after three quarters, with no persistent response. Seasonal adjustment is therefore not driving the official result. We also test for residual seasonality in the X-13-adjusted series: joint $F$ tests on quarterly dummies do not reject the null of no residual seasonality for any official variable (all $p$-values between 0.90 and 1.00). The broader fiscal measure alone does not restore the leakage (Hybrid-F), interpolated outcomes alone do not restore it (Hybrid-O), and joint interpolation in the common window does not restore it (Interpolated both). The broad official measures that include public investment and development partners also produce near-zero import responses and a negative GDP response after three quarters (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}).

The five-variable official model is unstable, but its point responses are also small and short-lived. We do not interpret them because the companion roots exceed one. The instability is consistent with the short sample: adding the CPI and credit doubles the number of parameters without adding enough observations. The long sample supports the five-variable model, although its maximum modulus (0.999) is close to the unit circle; the cost is that the additional observations are interpolated and come from an earlier period.

The prior is not driving the sample contrast. The hierarchical posterior median of the overall tightness is 1.32 in the official sample, 0.22 in the long sample, and 1.94 in the long sample with two dummies. Fixing $\lambda$ on a grid from 0.05 to 3.0 leaves the long-sample impact response between 1.01\% and 1.04\%, and the official response between $-0.12\%$ and $-0.03\%$. The long sample with only two dummies is less stable: its impact response ranges from 0.09\% to 0.70\%, and its six-quarter response from 0.78\% to 2.04\%. The long-sample result is therefore not an artifact of a loose prior, but the two-dummy variant is prior-sensitive. An OLS-VAR benchmark with the same ordering and dummies estimates a long-sample impact response of 1.14\% and an official response of $-0.006\%$, close to the LP estimates. The BVAR shrinks the long-sample response toward zero (0.91\%) and makes the official response more negative ($-0.07\%$); the difference between estimators is a shrinkage effect, not a different sign or a different sample conclusion.

A Monte Carlo exercise shows that the near-zero official estimate is not merely a power failure. We simulate the official LP regression 1,000 times under the null and under known import responses of 1.2\%, 2.1\%, and 5.5\%. The empirical 5\% critical values are 2.78 at three quarters and 2.91 at six quarters, above the normal 1.96 because the HAC test over-rejects slightly in this small sample; calibrating size to 5\% removes that distortion. Power to detect a 1.2\% response is 1.00 at three quarters and 0.997 at six quarters. The implied 95\% minimum detectable effect is about 0.21\% at three quarters and 0.31\% at six quarters. The BVAR posterior implies a minimum detectable effect of 0.17\% and 0.06\% at the same horizons. The official data therefore rule out effects above roughly 0.3\%, but they cannot distinguish zero from small leakage below that threshold.

The first-difference specification provides an additional check on the levels model. Estimating the official three-variable system in log differences with the same prior and two lags yields a stable companion matrix (maximum modulus 0.581). Cumulative import responses remain small and slightly negative: $-0.05\%$ at impact, $-0.08\%$ after two quarters, $-0.07\%$ after five quarters, and $-0.08\%$ after eleven quarters, with intervals that include zero. The cumulative GDP response is positive and larger than in the levels model ($0.48\%$ at impact, $0.13\%$ after two quarters, $0.20\%$ after five quarters, and $0.22\%$ after eleven quarters), which shows that the output response is specification-sensitive. The import conclusion is unchanged: differencing does not restore the long-sample leakage.

The FEVD tells the same story. In the official three-variable model, the fiscal shock explains 1.5\% of import variance at the one-quarter horizon, 6.9\% after six quarters, and 7.1\% after twelve quarters; for GDP, the shares are 23.3\%, 24.3\%, and 24.4\%. In the long three-variable model, the fiscal shock explains 54.0\% of import variance at impact, 57.1\% after six quarters, and 54.6\% after twelve quarters; for GDP, the shares are 31.4\%, 22.3\%, and 18.3\%. The long-sample fiscal shock is a dominant source of import fluctuations; the official fiscal shock is not. Figure~\ref{fig:fevd} presents the FEVDs.

\subsection{Practical recommendation}
\label{sec:recommendation}

The results support a simple decision rule for applied work in data-limited economies. First, use indicator-based quarterly data for inference whenever they exist; these data retain independent high-frequency variation and can be tested directly. Second, treat long interpolated samples as historical benchmarks, not as policy counterfactuals. Univariate disaggregation can be useful for description and for extending a sample, but it should not be the sole basis for a structural leakage estimate. Third, when both types of data are available, report a common-window test that varies the fiscal series and the outcome series separately. A long-sample result that disappears in the common window should be interpreted as sample-window evidence, not as a stable structural parameter. Fourth, state how event dummies enter the model. In our application, the long-sample estimate is sensitive to this choice, and omitting or silently dropping the dummies changes the conclusion.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper revisited the evidence on fiscal leakage in Timor-Leste using the INETL quarterly indicator. The main conclusion is that the strong leakage result obtained from long interpolated data is fragile. With the INETL indicator for 2014Q1--2023Q4, local projections---our preferred baseline---estimate import responses between $-0.28\%$ and $0.24\%$ across horizons. Most intervals include zero, and the few precisely estimated values are small and alternate in sign. A stable three-variable BVAR, reported as a robustness check, estimates an import response of $-0.07\%$ at impact and essentially zero thereafter. The output response is not robust across specifications. The long 2002--2023 sample built by Denton--Cholette disaggregation produces a larger import response: the LP peaks at 5.5\% after three quarters, and the BVAR estimates 0.91\% at impact. The sample contrast is not due to ordering, seasonal adjustment, or prior tightness; the BVAR--LP gap is a shrinkage difference.

The methodological lesson is specific. Temporal disaggregation smooths the series and removes high-frequency independent variation, but shared smoothness alone does not generate the leakage: in the common 2014--2023 window, even the cell with interpolated fiscal and outcome series produces a near-zero import response. The long-sample result is associated with the earlier observations, but that subsample is stable only when the event dummies are included, and the timing and magnitude are sensitive to which dummies are used. The 2x2 design therefore shows that the earlier mechanism was too broad, while confirming that long interpolated samples can produce estimates that are not reproduced in a common quarterly window. Because the INETL indicator is itself benchmarked and disaggregated with quarterly indicators, the comparison isolates the incremental effect of univariate interpolation and the sample window, not the difference between raw and constructed data.

These findings have implications for policy analysis in data-limited economies. Estimates of fiscal multipliers and leakage based on long interpolated samples should be treated with caution, and they should be tested against indicator-based quarterly data and common-window designs whenever available. For Timor-Leste, the INETL data do not provide evidence of a large import leakage, but they also do not provide a precise estimate of the fiscal multiplier. The small import responses are consistent with the existing estimates of 0.08--0.17 for the multiplier \parencite{worldbank2021,imf2024}. The short sample, measurement error in the quarterly indicator, the volatility of government expenditure, and the source differences between national-accounts and administrative data all limit precision. The appropriate conclusion is not that leakage is absent, but that the available quarterly data do not support the large and persistent leakage estimated from the long interpolated sample.

Further work should extend the INETL sample as new quarterly observations become available, explore alternative fiscal shocks based on budget execution data, add a broad fiscal measure that includes public investment, and apply the common-window 2x2 design to other small open economies with both annual and indicator-based quarterly data.
